On the 2026–27 academic job market · available Fall 2027

I study how telework is reshaping metropolitan areas: who can trade commuting for cheaper or roomier housing, and who is left bearing the cost of location. My dissertation, advised by Casey Dawkins, follows this through housing costs, home–work separation, and commuting behavior.

I teach graduate quantitative and spatial methods, with an emphasis on helping students make, explain, and test their analytical choices (teaching statement). My applied work with public agencies keeps me focused on how analysis gets used.

Dissertation

Expected Spring 2027

The Unequal Price of Proximity: Telework, Commute Frequency, and Place-Tied Workers in U.S. Cities

Committee: Casey Dawkins (chair), Chester Harvey, John Park

Remote work lets some workers commute less often and live farther from their jobs, while leaving others unchanged. Across three essays, I show that this uneven repricing of distance has redistributed the burden of location, with consequences for affordability and for travel-demand forecasts built on stated commuting behavior.
